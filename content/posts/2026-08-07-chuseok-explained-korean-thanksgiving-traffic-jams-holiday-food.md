Chuseok — Korea's Thanksgiving — is the single biggest event on the Korean calendar, and if you're in Korea when it happens, you'll feel it in every direction. I still remember my first Chuseok in Seoul: the subway was eerily quiet, every restaurant in my neighborhood was shuttered, and then my Korean friend's mother handed me the most perfectly formed crescent-shaped rice cake I'd ever seen. That one moment told me more about Korean culture than months of language classes. In this guide I'll break down what chuseok actually is, why songpyeon matters so much, how the holiday unfolds day by day, and what you should know as a traveler or curious K-culture fan before this year's celebrations.

---

## What Is Chuseok? Korea's Harvest Thanksgiving Explained

Chuseok (추석) is a three-day harvest festival held on the 15th day of the 8th month of the lunar calendar — landing in late September or early October most years — and it is, without question, **the most important holiday in South Korea**. The name literally translates to "Autumn Eve," and it dates back at least to the Silla Dynasty (57 BCE–935 CE), where it may have roots in a month-long weaving competition called *Gabae*. The holiday is sometimes called *Hangawi* (한가위), meaning "the great middle of autumn."

The core idea is similar to North American Thanksgiving: families gather, give thanks for the harvest, share a huge meal, and honor those who came before them. But chuseok carries a deeply Confucian layer — the *charye* ancestral rite — that gives the holiday a spiritual weight you won't find in a turkey dinner. According to [Korea.net](https://www.korea.net/AboutKorea/Korean-Life/Chuseok), chuseok is one of Korea's two most celebrated holidays alongside Seollal (Lunar New Year), and both are official public holidays with three days off (as of August 2026).

In 2026, Chuseok falls on **October 3 (Saturday)**, with the official holiday period running October 2–4. Because October 3 is also National Foundation Day (*Gaecheonjeol*), check the official [Korea Tourism Organization (VisitKorea)](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr) site for the confirmed substitute holiday schedule, as these details can shift.

## The Great Korean Migration: Traffic Jams Like You've Never Seen

Every Chuseok, roughly 30–40 million individual trips are made across South Korea as people return to their hometowns — a phenomenon the Korean media calls *minjok daeidong* (민족 대이동), or "the great migration of the people." It is spectacular and slightly terrifying.

A drive from Seoul to Busan that normally takes about 4–4.5 hours can balloon to **9–11 hours** on the eve of Chuseok. Expressways become parking lots. KTX high-speed rail tickets sell out weeks, sometimes months, in advance. Bus terminals see overnight queues. I once watched a friend refresh the Korail ticketing page for 40 straight minutes at midnight the moment reservations opened — she got the last seat on a train to Gwangju.

**What this means for travelers:**
1. Book any intercity transport at least 4–6 weeks ahead if you're traveling during the holiday period.
2. If you're staying in Seoul, embrace the quiet — the city empties out and major attractions are blissfully uncrowded.
3. Most shops, restaurants, and markets will be **closed on Chuseok day itself** (October 3), so stock up the day before.
4. Convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven) are your best friend — they stay open and stock seasonal snacks.
5. Check expressway conditions via the [Korea Expressway Corporation](https://www.ex.co.kr/eng/) for real-time traffic data.

The flip side? If you're in the countryside for Chuseok, you get to witness Korea at its most warmly domestic — relatives spilling out of small houses, children in *hanbok*, the smell of food drifting from every window.

## Songpyeon and the Food That Defines the Holiday

No food is more synonymous with chuseok than **songpyeon (송편)** — half-moon-shaped rice cakes steamed on a bed of pine needles. If there's one thing to know about Korean Thanksgiving food, it's this: songpyeon is not just a snack, it's a ritual.

The dough is made from freshly harvested rice flour, tinted in natural colors (white, green from mugwort, pink from omija berry, yellow from gardenia) and filled with sesame seeds and honey, sweet red bean paste, or chestnut. The family gathers the night before Chuseok — traditionally called *Chuseok eve* — to shape them together. There's even a folk belief that a woman who makes beautiful songpyeon will have a beautiful daughter.

Here's a quick look at the main Chuseok foods you'll encounter:

| Food | Korean Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Rice cake | 송편 (Songpyeon) | Half-moon rice cakes steamed on pine needles |
| Taro soup | 토란국 (Toranguk) | Earthy soup made with taro root, a harvest staple |
| Japchae | 잡채 | Glass noodles stir-fried with vegetables & beef |
| Jeon (savory pancakes) | 전 | Pan-fried patties of fish, zucchini, or kimchi |
| Hangwa | 한과 | Traditional Korean confectionery, often gifted |
| Rice wine | 동동주 / 막걸리 | Milky fermented rice wine, the drink of harvest |

The *charye* (차례) table is set before dawn on Chuseok morning: rice, soup, grilled meats and fish, vegetables, fruits, songpyeon, and rice wine are all laid out in a prescribed order as an offering to ancestors. The family bows together. It's quiet and genuinely moving even as an outside observer.

## Chuseok Customs, Clothes, and Games You Should Know

Chuseok is one of the few occasions when Koreans of all generations willingly put on *hanbok* — Korea's traditional clothing — and the sight of families dressed in vivid silks and linens heading to ancestral graves is one I find genuinely beautiful every year.

**Key Chuseok customs at a glance:**

- **Seongmyo (성묘):** Families visit and tend to ancestral graves, clearing weeds and leaving food offerings. This usually happens on Chuseok morning after charye.
- **Ganggangsullae (강강술래):** A UNESCO-recognized circle dance performed under the full moon, traditionally by women in the southwestern regions. [UNESCO lists it as an Intangible Cultural Heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/ganggangsullae-00188) (as of August 2026).
- **Ssireum (씨름):** Traditional Korean wrestling, like sumo but with a distinct belt-grip technique. Televised ssireum tournaments are a Chuseok TV staple.
- **Yutnori (윷놀이):** A board game played with four wooden sticks — deceptively simple, surprisingly competitive.

If you're visiting Korea during Chuseok, many palaces and folk villages put on free or low-cost traditional performances. Gyeongbokgung Palace and the National Folk Museum of Korea in Seoul typically host holiday programs — check [VisitKorea's event calendar](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/enu/EVT/SO_EN_3.jsp) before you go, as schedules are confirmed closer to the date.

## Chuseok Gift Culture: What Koreans Give (and Why It's a Big Deal)

One aspect of chuseok that surprises most international visitors is the sheer scale of gift-giving. Department stores dedicate entire floors to Chuseok gift sets from July onward. Corporate gift budgets run into the billions of won. According to the Korea Customs Service and retail industry data (as of August 2026), **spam gift sets** remain one of the top-selling Chuseok gifts — a legacy of post-war scarcity when processed meat was a luxury.

Today's popular gift tiers look roughly like this:

| Price Range (KRW) | Typical Gift |
|---|---|
| ₩20,000–50,000 | Spam/tuna sets, cooking oil sets, snack boxes |
| ₩50,000–150,000 | Korean beef (hanwoo) sets, premium fruit, red ginseng |
| ₩150,000–500,000+ | High-end hanwoo, premium seafood, luxury confectionery |

The etiquette: gifts are usually given to parents, in-laws, close mentors, or bosses. It is totally normal to receive a gift box at work. Refusing a gift can feel rude; accept with two hands and a slight bow.

## Traveling in Korea During Chuseok: Practical Tips for 2026

The honest reality is that Chuseok is **both a magical time and a logistically tricky one** to be a tourist in Korea. Here's how to make the most of it.

**Before the holiday (October 1–2):**
- Hit the traditional markets (Gwangjang Market in Seoul, Jagalchi in Busan) — they are alive with holiday prep energy and food.
- Buy any groceries or supplies you need; closures start the evening of October 2.

**On Chuseok day (October 3):**
- Major tourist sites like Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, and Bukchon Hanok Village are often open and dramatically less crowded than usual — a rare window.
- Public transport (Seoul Metro, buses) runs on a holiday schedule — check the [Seoul Metro official site](https://www.seoulmetro.co.kr/en/) for updates.
- Expect most local restaurants to be closed; hotel restaurants and convenience stores are your go-to.

**After the holiday (October 5 onward):**
- Seoul wakes up again fast. Markets reopen, restaurants are packed, and life snaps back to full speed.

## Your Quick Chuseok Cheat Sheet

**Key takeaways to save and share:**

- ✅ **Chuseok 2026** falls on October 3, with the holiday period running October 2–4 (verify substitute holiday at [VisitKorea](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr)).
- ✅ **Songpyeon** (half-moon pine-steamed rice cakes) is the defining food — try making it at a cultural center if you're in Korea.
- ✅ Book **KTX/intercity buses 4–6 weeks early** if you need to travel during the holiday.
- ✅ **Chuseok day itself** is quiet in cities — use it to visit palaces with zero crowds.
- ✅ Gift sets (especially spam and fruit) are a completely normal and appreciated gesture for Korean colleagues or hosts.
- ✅ Ganggangsullae circle dances and ssireum wrestling are worth seeking out for a full cultural experience.
- ✅ Prices, schedules, and public holiday substitution days change — always confirm on official government or venue pages before your trip.

Chuseok is one of those rare moments when modern, fast-moving Korea pauses, exhales, and looks backward with genuine affection. Whether you're stuck in the traffic or lucky enough to be at a family table eating fresh songpyeon, there is nothing quite like it.
